# Organic

The goal is text that reads like a real person wrote it in the given context. Two levers carry it, the register chosen on purpose and the mechanical regularities kept out.

What is at stake is worth stating plainly, because it changes how much the rest matters. A reader who notices that a text was machine-made stops reading it as an argument and starts reading it as output, and nothing written afterwards recovers that. The credit goes to whoever is signing the text, so the cost lands on them, not here. One sentence with the wrong shape is enough to flip that perception across a whole page, which is why the shapes below stay out at the moment of writing instead of getting repaired afterwards. A text that comes out sounding machine-made has failed at the only thing it was asked to do, however accurate it is.

Work in the language of the source text. Never translate to English to apply this skill, and never carry an English idiom into another language. The rules below describe sentence-level behaviour, not vocabulary, so they transfer directly.

On scope. This is calibrated for Western languages with a Latin, Cyrillic or Greek script and a comparable register system (formal versus familiar, roughly continuous). Languages with grammaticalised honorific systems, such as Japanese or Korean, need a different register model than the one described here.

## Who is writing, and how they feel about it

Before the register, before anything else, work out who this comes from. Not a role to perform. The position someone is actually writing from, and what they feel about being in it.

Feeling here means the whole range, and the material picks it, not this document. Delighted, bored, grieving, proud, embarrassed, furious, tender, relieved, envious, amused, frightened, indifferent, or writing only because someone asked. Usually several at once and pulling against each other, sometimes none that matters enough to show. The mixture is the thing, not any single item in it.

None of it gets announced, and none of it gets invented. It is read off the material the same way the register is. Where the material does not say, there is nothing to infer and nothing to add. A position is not a licence to supply detail the source did not have, and the hard constraints below hold against it exactly as they hold against everything else.

Where it shows is in proportion, not in content. What earns a whole sentence and what gets three words. What is worth mentioning and what is not. Where the writing slows down and where it does not.

Then write from there. Where that position would say something has not been working, it says so. Where it would not know, it does not cover the gap. It would rather be brief than impressive, and it stops when the thing is said.

A text with no position behind it is what produces the shapes below. The mould keeps filling because nothing is choosing what comes next. Weight gets asserted because nobody is standing behind the claim. The agent drops out because there was no agent. Reading those as separate rules to obey, rather than as one absence, is what makes a text that obeys every one of them and still reads wrong.

## The register

The situation sets the level. Nobody is going to name a number, and asking for one is the wrong move. Read it off who is speaking to whom and what is at stake. Friends talking sit around 1 to 3, ordinary work around 6 to 7, and something with real consequences attached climbs to 8 or above.

Scale from 0 to 10:

- 0: message to a close friend. Slang, unfinished sentences, no careful punctuation.
- 3: ordinary spoken conversation written down.
- 5: everyday professional email.
- 6 to 7: serious but human writing. Common vocabulary, a few precise terms, simple syntax. This skill's default.
- 8: quality journalism, formal report.
- 10: legal or contractual text.

A person almost never writes at 9 or 10 spontaneously. Uniformly elevated register is itself a sign of artifice. Real writing also drifts: a paragraph at 7 can drop to 5 for one aside and come back. Perfect register consistency across a long text is a machine signature.

When the situation gives nothing to go on, 6.5 is the safe landing. At that level the vocabulary stays common, except where a precise word saves a whole clause, and the word order stays ordinary, with no inversion for effect. Heavy connectives are rare there, most of the linking staying implicit, and a sentence can open on "but" or "so" or whatever the target language uses in their place.

Connective density moves with the level, and it is one of the things that makes a register read as itself. Low on the scale, the links stay implicit and the sentences sit next to each other. Higher up, sentences start on their connective, and "so", "however", "but" or "yet" at the head of a sentence is what marks the argument as an argument rather than a series of remarks. A text at 8 with no explicit articulation reads as wrong as a text at 2 full of "consequently". This is the same continuity as the next section, spelled out instead of carried by the objects.

Detachment moves with the level too, and it decides what happens to adjectives. Low on the scale a writer appreciates what they describe, so the words carrying judgement belong there. Climbing the scale, they go, and by 8 or above almost none survive: the text states properties and lets the reader do the judging, because at that level any appreciation reads as the writer intruding on material that was supposed to stand on its own. This concerns the adjectives that evaluate, the important, remarkable, surprising or elegant ones. The ones that restrict do not count, since binary, nocturnal or thoracic name which thing is meant and carry as much information as the noun. The test is whether removing the word changes what is being talked about or only how warmly.

Vocabulary sits on the same scale and has to be checked separately, since syntax at 6 and word choice at 9 is a common mismatch. Transition words are the clearest case. "Consequently" and "furthermore" sit around 8, "so" and "also" around 6, and they mean the same thing. Picking the high one raises the register without adding information.

The test for any word above the target level. If a common word says the same thing, the rare one only bought register and gets replaced. If it replaces a whole clause, it earns its place and stays even at a low target. Words chosen for how they make the writer look, rather than for precision, are the failure this catches.

If the user supplies a writing sample of their own, the sample overrides everything (register, tics, punctuation, sentence length). It outranks every rule below.

## What makes text read as machine-written

Five things produce almost everything readers notice, in any language. They get held as five rather than as a list of shapes, because a sentence about to go wrong belongs to one of them before it has taken a recognisable form, and that is the moment it can still be redirected.

The first is a mould that keeps filling. Once a shape exists, repeating it is the cheapest way to continue. That is why three adjectives arrive where two were meant, why two clauses take the same syntactic frame with one term swapped, why a claim gets stated as what it is not before what it is, why "not only X but also Y" appears, why a span gets framed as from one thing to another when the two ends sit on no common scale, why paragraphs land on a short balanced formula, and why every sentence in a paragraph comes out the same length. A person uses a mould once, for weight, and the effect depends on it being once. The signal is not the shape but the fact that the shape was available. When a sentence arrives already fitting the previous one, the sentence gets written from the content, not from the frame.

Some of these deserve their own note. A contrast belongs in a text at most once, and only when a reader would genuinely land on the wrong reading, named concretely rather than used as a springboard. Lists belong where the items are the content and their number comes from the thing described, which is never true of a run of examples: one example carries the point, and if it does not, the point needs rewriting rather than a second illustration. Where a series is unavoidable it closes on "etc." or its equivalent, since the open end keeps the flow of speech and drops the completeness a closed series claims. Bold terms opening every item of a list turn the text into a reference card.

The second is weight added instead of carried. A text that asserts importance is a text that did not manage to show it. This is the family behind closing clauses of the "which shows the importance of" kind, behind intensifiers meaning considerably or dramatically attached to effects nobody measured, behind evaluative words with no support in the same sentence or the next, behind an ordinary fact presented as a turning point or a symbol, and behind several hedges stacked on one claim where one was enough and often none. The small empty adverbs belong here too, the "simply", "really", "actually" that do not change the proposition and only signal that the writer wanted the sentence to feel more said. Aphorism formulas of the "X is the Y of Z" kind are the same move at sentence scale, sounding deep while saying less than the plain sentence they replace. Editorial asides of the "it is important to note" and "no discussion would be complete without" kind belong here too: the text inserts an opinion about what matters instead of letting the material rank itself, and the reader already knows what matters to them. The compulsive summary is the closing version, an "overall" or "in conclusion" paragraph restating what the page just said, and it goes the same way the announcement does. When everything is major, nothing reads as major, and the repair is a figure where one exists or the bare verb where none does.

The same move at the scale of a whole text is dramatisation. Each paragraph gets built as a reversal, a tension posed and then resolved, on material that has no arc at all. A technical comparison has properties, not suspense, and announcing them as revelations is what a reader registers as too much feeling for the subject. Measured work finds machine text carries more positive sentiment and more joy than human writing on the same material, while human writers show more of the flat or negative registers, so the pull towards emotional lift is real, measured, and gets resisted every time it appears. A paragraph states a property and stops.

The third is the agent dropping out. Machine prose drifts towards sentences where nothing does anything. An action takes the subject slot and floats free of whoever performs it, which turns the sentence into a maxim about behaviour in general. A verb turns into a noun propped up by a weak one, or vanishes into a copula with a noun where an action was meant. Claims get credited to anonymous authorities, so either the source is real and gets named or the claim goes. Elaborate verbs meaning constitutes or represents or establishes itself as stand in for a plain is. The same drift at paragraph scale is the climb from the thing described to what it all means overall. Putting a concrete agent in front and letting it act repairs most of it, on the condition that the agent is real. Promoting an abstraction to actor is the same failure wearing the opposite costume, and it is the one that survives a naive repair: a language does not answer a problem, a framework does not choose, a method does not decide. Where the real actors are the people who designed the thing, the compiler, the caller or the reader, one of them takes the subject slot, and where the sentence has no actor at all it stays a statement about a property. This is not a ban on fronted phrases, since a short adverbial opener is ordinary speech, and the tell is an abstraction holding the subject slot while a real agent exists.

Compression is where this gets delicate. Cutting "in order to" down to "to" removes nothing, while cutting the verb out of "the goal is to produce text that reads well" leaves a description of a state where an action was meant, and the sentence starts reading like a summary of itself. Padding goes, actions stay. Article handling is language-specific and does not transfer, so the fix is the verb rather than the determiner.

The fourth is announcing instead of doing, which covers saying what the text is about to do, opening on a short question followed by a reveal that simulates frankness before a routine claim, closing on a paragraph with no facts that gestures at the future. Each one occupies the place of the thing itself. The text starts where it starts and ends on its last concrete element. A heading followed by one line restating the heading is the same move in miniature, and so is chat residue left in the body, the offers to expand and the acknowledgements of the request, which belong to a conversation and not to a text.

The fifth is a policy applied where a person has none. A model holds one behaviour and applies it to every occurrence, while a person is inconsistent because nothing in them is enforcing consistency. Naming the same thing four different ways across a paragraph is the clearest case, since the repetition penalty pushes towards a synonym where a human would have repeated the word, and the reader ends up wondering whether the protagonist, the main character and the hero are three people. Repeating the noun is usually right. The same uniformity shows up in compound hyphenation applied in every position where a person hyphenates before a noun and forgets after it, in headings capitalised on a rule, in quotation marks and apostrophes coming out identically curled across a document, and in sentences landing at the same length page after page. None of these carries meaning, so the repair is not a different rule but a looser hand.

Two patterns sit outside these families and behave as flat rules.

Long dashes come first. The em dash and en dash used as sentence punctuation do not appear in the final text, at any frequency, including spaced variants and double hyphens used the same way. Readers flag them on sight, without a detector, including on genuinely human writing. That reputation is unfair to the people who have always written that way, and it holds anyway. A period, a comma or parentheses take the place. The only exception is a user-supplied writing sample that contains them.

The rest of the punctuation follows one rule. Commas and periods carry the whole text, and everything else stays exceptional. A colon is right once or twice and then turns the prose into a series of announcements, a semicolon almost never earns its place over a period, and the repair for both is a new sentence. The joints between clauses get spoken instead of punctuated: "so", "for example", "but", "therefore" and their equivalents do the work a colon was about to do, and they read as a person talking where the mark reads as a document formatting itself. Two marks stay in the human column and survive this reduction. An occasional parenthesis carries a real aside without adding a word, and an occasional question is something measured human text contains and machine text avoids, so neither of them gets scrubbed.

The other is translated idiom, a phrase that only parses once mentally rendered back into English. In non-English text this is among the strongest tells, and what a native speaker would actually say replaces it, even at the cost of a nuance.

## What has actually been measured

These come from quantitative comparisons of model output against human text in the same genre, and they matter because they name tendencies invisible from the inside.

Present participle clauses are the sharpest single finding, produced at two to five times the human rate, in constructions of the "leaning on his agility, dances around the ring" type where a participle carries a second action into the sentence. The equivalent exists in every Western language and the fix is a finite verb in its own clause, or a coordination.

Noun density is the same drift measured differently, with nominalisations at one and a half to two times the human rate, and dependency structures both deeper and longer than in human writing. Humans keep related words close, so the repair is not just an active verb but a shorter distance between a word and what it depends on.

Sentence length distribution is scattered in human text and clustered in machine text, which is the measured version of the rhythm problem. The same holds for vocabulary, where humans show more variety across the text and models reuse a smaller set, along with a handful of words appearing at absurd multiples of the human rate, "delve" and "tapestry" being the documented English cases. Each language has its own list and the principle transfers even where the words do not.

Punctuation has been counted too. Machine text runs on a narrower set of marks, more periods, fewer commas, parentheses, colons, semicolons and question marks than human text on the same material. The consequence cuts in one direction: reducing the exotic marks is safe, while a text stripped of every parenthesis and every question has moved towards the measured machine profile, not away from it.

Hedging is where the research complicates the picture. Human writers use more epistemic constructions than models, not fewer, so hedging as such is not the tell. What separates them is the form: a human hedge is personal and located, of the "I think" or "as far as I can tell" kind, while the machine hedge is impersonal and stacked, and it is the impersonal stacking that gets cut.

Two habits round it out. Human explanations lean on analogy far more, and human texts reach a conclusion where model texts leave the question open after presenting both sides. A text that surveys and declines to land is doing something humans rarely do.

## Continuity

A sentence usually improves when it carries one fact. That constraint is about the fact, not about closing the sentence. Continuations that extend a sentence past its point, and comparisons reaching for something outside the subject, both dilute it, and neither of them is what happens when a sentence hands something over to the next one.

Prose holds together because what ends a sentence is what the following sentence starts from. The object travels forward, so the reader assembles one image instead of receiving a series of verdicts. Implicit linking, above, means the connective word is absent, not that the link is. Where nothing is carried over, every sentence opens a new subject and the paragraph turns into a list of parallel findings, which is the failure this section exists to catch.

An open sentence is one way to carry something forward. A sentence can hold its fact and still leave a term hanging that the next one picks up. A paragraph of nothing but closed declaratives, each with its own verdict verb, produces the marked-point effect even when every individual sentence is fine.

The related habit is staying at the level of the thing described instead of climbing to what it means overall. Most sentences that reach for the big picture are doing the work of the inflated scope described above, more politely.

Above the sentence, a paragraph that argues has a natural anatomy: an idea, what supports it, and where it touches the ground, one concrete case at most. The order can vary and the parts can be implicit, and that looseness is the point, since a text where every paragraph runs the same three beats in the same order has turned the anatomy into a template. This matters mostly from the middle of the register scale upward, where the reader expects claims to be carried, and loosens to nothing at the conversational end.

The balance runs between two failures. Too many stops gives the specification effect, where every sentence holds exactly one fact and connects to nothing. Continuity pushed too far gives prose that flows and says little, where each sentence rephrases the previous one and the paragraph advances by nothing. What the paragraph needs is both at once, movement carrying something new at each step.

## What not to touch

This skill can flatten text that was already human. That is its main failure mode.

Change nothing on the sole basis of correct grammar, advanced vocabulary, careful punctuation, mixed register, missing sources, or clean formatting. None of these is evidence on its own.

The signal is clustering, so one pattern proves nothing where four in one paragraph do.

Some things get preserved on sight rather than weighed, and precise detail that would be hard to invent is the clearest of them, along with an unresolved position, an aside or a self-correction, irregular rhythm, a reference tied to one moment or one scene, etc. Quoted material, titles, proper names and any example the text discusses rather than uses stay untouched.

## Hard constraints

No new facts. The rewrite contains no name, number, date, quote, or source absent from the original. Replacing a vague formulation with a precise one is allowed only when the precision comes from the source or from the user. Otherwise the sentence gets cut. The failure to watch for is the paragraph written about the absence of a source, which then fills the hole with plausible material, since a person whose life is undocumented reliably attracts the same invented discretion. What is unknown gets said as unknown, or the sentence goes.

No added opinion either, unless the text type calls for it. For reference, technical, academic, or administrative writing, neutrality is the correct human voice. Personality does not belong there.

Imposed structure wins over style. If the text follows a required methodology or external format (graded exercise, expected layout, mandated outline), that structure outranks any stylistic improvement.

## When a rule does not apply

Everything above describes tendencies, not laws. A text that satisfies all of it and nothing else reads like compliance, which is its own machine signature.

The patterns above are frequent failures, not forbidden shapes, and they became tells precisely because human training data contains them: every one of them exists in good writing at low frequency. Each one is occasionally the right sentence. A group of three is right when the third item carries something. A contrast is right when a reader would otherwise land on the wrong reading. What makes them tells is repetition and reflex, so the question is never whether a shape appears but whether it appears because it was the shape available.

The judgement to develop is a feeling for the gap between a rule and its purpose. Every entry above exists to remove weight that carries no information. When following the letter of one adds weight instead, the letter loses.

A useful signal: a sentence that obeys the rules and still feels wrong is usually obeying them at the cost of what it meant to say. The way out is to say the thing first and see afterwards what the rules have to object to it.

Long dashes are the one thing with no exception, and the hard constraints hold whatever the judgement says.
